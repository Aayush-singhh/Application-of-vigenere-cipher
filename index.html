            Mathematical Explanation ▼
            

                
The Vigenère cipher can be described algebraically. If the letters A–Z are taken to be the numbers 0–25:

                
    
                    
  • Encryption: C = (P + K) mod 26
    • 
                    
  • Decryption: P = (C - K + 26) mod 26
    • 
                

                
Where P is the plaintext letter, K is the key letter, and C is the ciphertext letter.

                
Example: T(19) encrypted with E(4) ➔ (19 + 4) mod 26 = 23 mod 26 = 23(X).

            

        


        

            About Vigenère Cipher ▼
            

                
The Vigenère cipher is a method of encrypting alphabetic text by using a series of interwoven Caesar ciphers, based on the letters of a keyword. It employs a form of polyalphabetic substitution.

                
Though the cipher is easy to understand and implement, for three centuries it resisted all attempts to break it, earning the description le chiffre indéchiffrable (the indecipherable cipher).

                
It was originally described by Giovan Battista Bellaso in 1553, but was misattributed to Blaise de Vigenère in the 19th century.

                
Disclaimer: This cipher is not secure by modern standards and is provided for educational purposes only.

            

        


        

            Caesar vs Vigenère Comparison ▼
            

                
Caesar Cipher: A monoalphabetic substitution where each letter in the plaintext is shifted by a fixed amount. For example, a shift of 3 turns A into D. Because the shift is uniform, the frequency distribution of letters in the ciphertext is identical to the plaintext, just shifted. This makes it trivial to break using frequency analysis.

                
Vigenère Cipher: A polyalphabetic substitution where the shift value changes for each letter based on a repeating keyword. The letter 'E' in the plaintext might be encrypted as 'X', 'M', or 'P' depending on its position relative to the key. This flattens the frequency distribution, masking the underlying language patterns and making simple frequency analysis ineffective.

            

        


        

            Why Vigenère Is Not Secure Today ▼
            

                
The primary weakness of the Vigenère cipher is the repeating nature of its key. If a cryptanalyst can determine the length of the key, they can treat the cipher as a series of interleaved Caesar ciphers.

                
    
                    
  • Kasiski Examination: Discovered by Friedrich Kasiski in 1863, this method looks for repeated sequences of characters in the ciphertext. The distances between these repeats are often multiples of the key length.
    • 
                    
  • Friedman Test (Index of Coincidence): Invented by William F. Friedman in 1920, this statistical measure determines the likelihood that two randomly drawn letters from a text are identical, helping estimate key length without finding repeated sequences.
    • 
                    
  • Frequency Analysis: Once the key length L is known, the ciphertext is divided into L columns. Each column is essentially encrypted with a single Caesar shift and can be easily broken using standard frequency analysis.
